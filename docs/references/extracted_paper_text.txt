Abstract 
It is essential to comprehend how the environment and climate vary. The geophysical data of 
NASA under the mission of soil moisture active passive (SMAP) have shown to be a valuable 
resource, offering hitherto undiscovered insights into the dynamics of soil moisture, and associated 
geophysical terms. To evaluate the effect of global warming on climatic changes, analysis of the 
SMAP has been conducted in several regions of Gujarat, India, over the last four years (2020-
2023) in the current study. After preprocessing the original dataset for missing value imputation, 
significant features with a greater influence on the surface soil moisture were extracted using a 
Random Forest (RF). A model for predicting soil moisture was constructed utilizing the selected 
features and the gradient boosting (GB) technique. The Gradient Boosting model achieved 
outstanding predictive accuracy (R² = 0.99 with minimal error), consistently surpassing the 
performance of other methods, including Lasso Regression, Linear Regression, and Random 
Forest. Though RF regressor was higher computational resource consuming than GB. The 
suggested predictive model has a lot of practical applications, including drought and climate 
monitoring.
Keywords: SMAP; Soil moisture; Regression; Gradient Boosting 
1. Introduction 
Water is a very necessary element of the planet, playing a central role in sustaining ecosystems 
and human development. However, increasing urbanization and desertification continue to pose 
serious challenges to its availability and management (Abahussain et al., 2002). With the global 
population expected to reach 9.725 billion by 2050 (Wang & Moriarty, 2018), the demand for both 
food and water is intensifying.  Efficient water resource management is now more critical than 
ever. Soil moisture plays a key role in this context. It acts as a temporary water reserve that directly 
supports both agriculture and climate regulation. Accurate knowledge of soil moisture levels is 
essential for tasks like irrigation scheduling (Hatfield et al., 2001; Gu et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2021), 
but conventional methods such as gravimetric sampling, tensiometers, neutron probes, and 

--- PAGE 4 ---
3
reflectometry tools (Romano, 2014; Dobriyal et al., 2012; Babaeian et al., 2019) are often invasive, 
time-consuming, providing partial spatial coverage. To overcome these limitations, remote sensing 
and satellite-based techniques have emerged as transformative solutions over the past few decades 
(Singh & Gaurav, 2023; Müllerová et al., 2021). Specifically, surface soil moisture (0-5 cm depth) 
and root zone moisture (0-200 cm depth) are now measurable at large scales using satellite 
missions such as NASA’s Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP) (SMAP, 2015; McColl et al., 
2017). SMAP employs both microwave radiometer and synthetic aperture radar (SAR) 
technologies. These instruments enable the mission to capture global-scale soil moisture 
variations, including areas where ground access is challenging (Renju & Raju, 2023; Yueh, 2000). 
Recent developments in artificial intelligence have further advanced soil moisture modeling (Singh 
et al., 2025). Various well-known learning models like CNNs and ConvLSTM have been used to 
analyze satellite images and understand complex moisture-related patterns in soil (Xu et al., 2021; 
Liu et al., 2022). The earlier models are often computationally intensive when applying to 
environmental policy or agricultural planning. To address these challenges, the study provides an 
option which is more efficient and interpretable. An ensemble learning framework that combines 
Gradient Boosting (GB) Regressor and Random Forest (RF) has been utilized for surface soil 
moisture prediction. Rather than relying on image data, this model uses structured tabular data 
derived from SMAP’s geophysical products. Covering the period from 2020 to 2023 and focusing 
on January, March, May, and August months that represent seasonal variation, this dataset captures 
soil-climate interactions across winter, summer, and monsoon periods. The selected study sites in 
Gujarat, India, include regions with diverse climatic and geographic characteristics such as semi-
arid zones, coastal plains, industrial hubs, and arid landscapes. This diversity in the dataset 
enhances model generalization and provides a robust testing ground for the proposed method. By 
focusing on computationally lightweight, feature-driven modeling, this work aims to bridge the 
gap between scientific accuracy and real-world usability in soil moisture prediction (Lakra et al., 
2024). A notable strength of this study is the way it integrates RF for identifying key features with 
GBR to carry out the final prediction of soil moisture. This two-step strategy allows for both 
interpretability and precision. The training of model is done on the tabular data of NASA SMAP 
mission, covering four different months across multiple seasons from 2020 to 2023. Also, data is 
collected from seven locations in Gujarat including Bhuj, Idar, Ankleshwar, Bhavnagar, Jamnagar, 
Vapi, and Palanpur, each offering a unique environmental and climatic profile. This level of spatial 
and temporal variability makes the proposed framework flexible and adaptable across a wide range 
of conditions.

3. Methodology
3.1 SMAP Dataset  
An open-source data of the satellite mission by NASA, SMAP (SMAP, 2015) clearly shown 
in Table A2 (Appendix A) is selected for the present study. Specially, SMAP-L4 global 3-hourly 
9 KM EASE-grid surface and root zone soil moisture geophysical data V007 has been selected. 
SMAP is a NASA initiative aimed at offering observations of soil moisture, which plays a 
significant role in study for soil moisture monitoring and its prediction. This dataset employs active 
radiometer as well as passive microwave measurements to estimate soil moisture levels on the 
earth’s surface. The SMAP L4_SM data is prepared using satellite observations by SMAP mission 
as well as land surface observation by Goddard Earth Observing System Model, Version 5 (GEOS-
5). Consequently, while surface soil moisture is directly observed by the SMAP radiometer, other 
geophysical variables such as rootzone moisture, soil temperatures at different depths, and runoff 
fluxes are derived through model simulations. This hybrid approach ensures a comprehensive and 
physically consistent dataset suitable for large-scale soil moisture analysis (Reichle, 2014; 
Rouzegari et al., 2025). The SMAP has been validated through multiple independent studies, which 
supports its reliability for large-scale soil moisture analysis (Lu et al., 2023; Albano et al., 2024; 
Wang et al., 2025). The SMAP-L4 global dataset includes geophysical, spatial, and temporal

information essential for soil moisture prediction. The geophysical variables include soil moisture, 
land surface temperature, and vegetation parameters, while spatial features such as coordinate grids 
are represented through latitude, longitude, and projection data. A full list of the dataset fields and 
their descriptions is provided in Table A2 (Appendix A). Despite this, because geographical data 
contains all the features that affect soil moisture, it is employed in the subsequent analysis. SMAP 
uses a satellite that gathers eight datasets of a single place every three hours. Each dataset consists 
of 45 columns (each column representing features affecting soil moisture and value of soil 
moisture) and 6262144 rows (each row representing unique image information). The soil moisture 
surface (sm_surface) has 4577419 null rows (SMAP, 2015). Consequently, the analysis has only 
made use of 1684725 rows and 45 columns of datasets of January, March, May, and August months 
of 2020, 2021, 2022 and 2023 for seven locations. Additionally, one dataset is chosen at random 
each month, for a total of 16 datasets collected from a single site. In the current study, a total of 
112 datasets has been collected and examined across 7 locations. 
3.2 Data Preprocessing
The first stage in using SMAP is data preparation. Managing and inputting the missing data is 
one of the primary responsibilities of the preprocessing step (Hegazi et al., 2021). If missing values 
are not handled appropriately, a bias could be introduced into the knowledge (Wang and Singh, 
2021; Chen et al., 2020). Instances of null or absent values frequently occur in geophysical data 
sets. It was crucial to address this challenge to ensure the quality of input data (Emmanuel et al., 
2021). To systematically resolve this issue, this study has developed a Python script that utilizes 
the h5py module to use HDF5 files, which are commonly employed for data storage. The algorithm 
implemented in this study is designed to analyze SMAP files, which contain critical geophysical 
information. The subsequent items delineate the primary responsibilities executed by the script.
•The datasets are efficiently preprocessed by the script through the repeated management of 
multiple dataset files via a list of file paths, where each path represents a distinct dataset file. 
•The main goal was to particularly deal with null values in the 'sm_surface' variable, which is 
important for soil moisture prediction. Null values in geophysical datasets are commonly 
represented as -9999.0. To solve this issue, script does the following.
➢A boolean mask named 'valid_rows_mask' is generated, which identifies rows where 
'sm_surface' values do not equal -9999.0, effectively marking rows with valid data. 
Consequently, rows with missing 'sm_surface' values are systematically removed from 
the dataset. 
➢The script offers valuable feedback throughout processing, displaying the structure of 
the dataset, the mask of valid rows, and alerts indicating the removal of null rows from 
each processed file.
•Further in rest of the features other than the target ‘sm_surface’, mean imputation is used to 
impute the missing value by performing column-wise mean imputation using Eq. 1.

3.3 Embedded Approach of Feature Selection
Data quality affects the performance of any machine learning model. Even the advance model 
cannot perform well if the data is noisy (Hegazi et al., 2021). A SMAP-L4 dataset initially 
contained 44 environmental variables (Appendix Table A1). To enhance soil moisture predictive 
capacity, an embedded feature selection method based on RF regression was employed. An 
exhaustive investigation into feature significance was conducted using the RF regressor, a robust 
ensemble learning method that combines multiple decision trees to improve predictive accuracy.

objective of the embedded feature selection was to systematically identify the most influential 
environmental parameters affecting surface soil moisture prediction. Initially, all the 44 features 
present in the geophysical dataset were used to train the RF Regressor model. The importance 
score of features was computed based on the Ginni importance criterion, which evaluates the 
contribution of each feature towards reducing prediction error. RF is particularly effective in

estimators (n_estimators = 100), no restriction on tree depth (max_depth = None), minimum two 
samples required for a split (min_samples_split = 2), and square root feature sampling 
(max_features = 'sqrt'). Random state was not set during feature selection to allow natural 
randomness in tree formation. A schematic diagram of embedded feature selection method (Liu 
et al., 2019) is shown in Fig. 2. Based on the calculated importance scores, the top 14 features 
were selected.

3.4 Gradient Boosting Regression for soil moisture prediction
 The Gradient Boosting (GB) Regressor was employed to predict the surface soil moisture by 
using the 14 important features selected through the RF embedded feature selection method. GBR 
builds an ensemble model by sequentially adding decision trees, where each new tree minimizes 
the residual errors made by the previous ensemble (Rao et al., 2019). It is a combined model with 
enhanced stability and greater performance (Shrivastav & Jha, 2021). It uses the boosting 
algorithm to solve the regression problem.  GBR minimizes a loss function using gradient descent 
(Li et al., 2018). The prediction after adding M trees is mathematically represented as

The GBR model was implemented using the scikit-learn library (Pedregosa et al., 2011). 
Hyperparameter tuning was systematically conducted using the GridSearchCV function. The 
parameters optimization includes, number of trees (n_estimators = 100), learning rate 
(learning_rate = 0.1), tree depth (max_depth = 3), and random state (random_state = 42). Fig. 5 
shows the general architecture of the GBR model. Setting a shallow tree depth (max_depth = 3) 
helped prevent overfitting, along with the benefit of feature selection in reducing model 
complexity. The training and evaluation process followed an 80:20 split, where 80% of the 
available data samples were used for training and 20% were used for testing. A single GBR model 
was trained and tested on data of each seven locations (Bhuj, Idar, Ankleshwar, Bhavnagar, 
Jamnagar, Vapi, and Palanpur) independently then additionally saved model on each location is 
tested on different location data to cross validate model’s predictive ability. This cross-validation 
step helped to confirm that the model works well even when applied to different environmental 
conditions. Three common evaluation metrics, MSE, mean absolute error (MAE), and R-squared 
(R²) were used to assess how well the model performed.

4. Soil Moisture Prediction Process
The model generation process for assessing soil moisture levels using the SMAP geophysical 
dataset is explained as follows. The powerful GBR, which employs ensemble learning to measure 
typical interdependency present in the data, is the foundation of the suggested prediction model. 
Using weaker learning algorithms that have been separately taught from several models, the 
ensemble machine learning algorithm combines the findings in some way, usually by averaging 
all the predictions to produce an overall better prediction. This has proven to be effective, and a 
lot of study in the field of ensemble learning has been done on it (Hegazi et al., 2021). It is highly 
helpful to predict soil moisture when a subset of geophysical properties is chosen. When the 
model's hyperparameters are set appropriately, the model's performance can potentially increase 
dramatically. One technique for choosing a model's ideal hyperparameter is GridSearchCV 
(Ahmad et al., 2022). A comprehensive approach is employed to maximize the GBR

6. Discussion 
This study proposes a novel surface soil moisture prediction model by utilizing GB Regressor 
which is trained on NASA's SMAP dataset (SMAP, 2015). Unlike many previous approaches over 
last 3 to 5 years that rely on satellite imagery and deep learning models like ConvLSTM or CNNs 
(Liu et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021), our approach focuses on structured tabular 
data. This model selection increases computational efficiency and model interpretability, which is 
very necessary in environmental applications where transparent decision-making is needed. The 
model achieved high accuracy across all seven selected regions in Gujarat, India. As shown in 
Table 3, the R² values consistently remained close to 0.99 m 3/m3, with MSE values on the order 
of 10⁻⁵ m3/m3. This is a clear indication of strong learning and generalization by the model. Key 
features such as sm_rootzone, sm_rootzone_wetness, and sm_surface_wetness stood out as 
dominant predictors. Their importance was established through RF-based feature selection ( Fig. 
8) and validated by the correlation analysis ( Fig. 4 ) (Speiser et al., 2019). To validate model 
performance spatially, predictions were tested across diverse locations—coastal (Bhavnagar, 
Jamnagar), industrial (Ankleshwar, Vapi), semi-arid (Bhuj), and arid (Palanpur). The soil moisture 
prediction plots for each location are shown in Figs. 12 (a)-(f). The model’s predictive curves align 
well with actual soil moisture values in most regions. However, for locations like Bhuj ( Fig. 12 
(a)), Ankleshwar (Fig. 12 (c)), and Jamnagar (Fig. 12 (e)), moderate divergence was observed in 
the mid-range moisture band (0.2–0.6). This can be explained by the unpredictable or dynamic 
weather conditions in these regions, as outlined in Table 1, including industrial interference or 
extreme semi-arid climates. These unpredictable signs show a need for more adaptive or hybrid 
modeling in such zones (Bentéjac et al., 2021). A comparative performance evaluation with other 

--- PAGE 24 ---
23
regression models was conducted and visualized in Fig. 11. While RF occasionally yielded slightly 
better MSE or MAE, its resource usage was notably higher, often crossing 10 GB in RAM during 
training resulting in kernel crashes on Google Colab. Whereas GBR consistently maintained strong 
accuracy (R² ≈  0.99 m 3/m3) with memory consumption of less than 8 GB. Thus, in practical 
applications, especially on constrained hardware, GBR provides a more reliable trade-off between 
performance and computational cost (Shrivastav & Jha, 2021; Reichle, 2014; Pedregosa et al., 
2011).  Other than that, GBR model emphasizes interpretability and reproducibility, a gap often 
found in deep learning approaches (Suganeshwari & Divya, 2024). Using an embedded RF feature 
selection strategy ( Fig. 2 ), users can trace which features influence the prediction. This 
transparency is crucial for scientific validation and policy implementation (Speiser et al., 2019). 
Additionally, our training procedure an 80: 20 train-test split and 5-fold cross-validation, as 
visualized in Fig. 6 ensures reproducibility and robust hyperparameter tuning (Ahmad et al., 2022; 
Emmanuel et al., 2021). The ability of models to perform consistently in the varying environment 
for varying time shows efficiency in accuracy, low resource usage and transparent modelling. But 
there are few conditions like excess rain, flood etc. which affect model performance much by 
reducing its predictive power. Despite strong results, the model has some limitations. In complex 
environmental zones (e.g., coastal, or industrial), standard geophysical features may not fully 
capture short-term fluctuations. Like too much rainfall or industrial discharge are difficult to model 
using only static features. Therefore, future work can focus on (i) integrating temporal satellite 
data or real-time meteorological features (Prasad et al., 2018), adopting transfer learning for 
regional adaptation (Li et al., 2021), or developing meta-ensemble models for dynamic regions 
(Rao et al., 2019).
7. Conclusion 
This study develops a highly accurate and reliable model using GBR to estimate soil moisture 
dynamics across seven diverse locations: Bhuj, Jamnagar, Bhavnagar, Vapi, Ankleshwar, 
Palanpur, and Idar. With an emphasis on four crucial testing months (January, March, May, and 
August), this study attempted to give a thorough understanding of soil moisture fluctuations across 
Gujarat's diverse geographic and climatic settings between 2020 and 2023. The GBR's remarkably 
low values of MSE and high values of R2 demonstrate its ability to capture intricate soil moisture 
fluctuations across these diverse regions and timeframes. The incorporation of the MAE value for 
GBR is particularly significant because it further demonstrates the model's ability to make accurate 
predictions while minimizing prediction errors. In the accurate prediction of soil 
moisture,'sm_rootzone,''sm_rootzone_wetness,' and 'sm_surface_wetness' have emerged as 
crucial determinants. They open the door to more complex and successful methods of predicting 
soil moisture in further studies. The accuracy and stability of GBR have the potential to completely 
change how water resources are managed, how agriculture is conducted, and how climate change 
adaptation plans are implemented.
